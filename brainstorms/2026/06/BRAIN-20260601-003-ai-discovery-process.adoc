## 1. Human Discussion

Here’s a brainstorm for an **AI Discovery Process** — a structured way to identify, evaluate, and prioritize AI opportunities before committing to full implementation.

The goal of AI discovery is to answer:

> “Where can AI create meaningful value, and what should we build first?”

A strong AI discovery process should avoid starting with technology for its own sake. Instead, it should begin with business problems, user needs, operational bottlenecks, data availability, and measurable outcomes.

---

### AI Discovery Process Overview

A practical AI discovery process can be broken into these phases:

1. **Context & Goals**
2. **Stakeholder Discovery**
3. **Workflow & Pain Point Mapping**
4. **AI Opportunity Identification**
5. **Data & Systems Assessment**
6. **Feasibility & Risk Review**
7. **Use Case Prioritization**
8. **Prototype / Pilot Definition**
9. **Roadmap & Business Case**

---

### 1. Context & Goals

Start by understanding the organization’s current situation.

Questions to ask:

- What business goals are we trying to support?
- Are we focused on cost reduction, revenue growth, speed, quality, personalization, compliance, or innovation?
- What departments are most interested in AI?
- What has already been attempted?
- Is leadership looking for internal productivity improvements or customer-facing AI products?

Example goals:

- Reduce customer support response time
- Automate document-heavy workflows
- Improve sales team efficiency
- Extract insights from large volumes of unstructured data
- Improve forecasting accuracy
- Enable employees to use internal knowledge more effectively

---

### 2. Stakeholder Discovery

Identify the people who understand the work, own the systems, and feel the pain.

Key stakeholder groups:

- Executives
- Department leaders
- Frontline employees
- Operations teams
- IT / engineering
- Data teams
- Legal / compliance
- Security
- Customer-facing teams

Discovery formats:

- Interviews
- Workshops
- Shadowing sessions
- Surveys
- Process walkthroughs
- Data/system reviews

The most useful conversations are often with people doing repetitive, manual, frustrating, or judgment-heavy work.

---

### 3. Workflow & Pain Point Mapping

Map current workflows before proposing AI.

For each workflow, capture:

- Trigger: What starts the process?
- Inputs: What information is needed?
- Steps: What happens manually or automatically?
- Decisions: Where is judgment required?
- Outputs: What is produced?
- Systems: What tools are involved?
- Exceptions: Where does work get stuck?
- Metrics: How is success measured?

Look for patterns like:

- Repetitive manual tasks
- Copy/paste between systems
- Large volumes of text, calls, tickets, emails, PDFs, or images
- Slow decision cycles
- Knowledge trapped in people’s heads
- Inconsistent quality
- Backlogs
- Compliance review burden
- High-cost expert review

---

### 4. AI Opportunity Identification

Once workflows and pain points are clear, translate them into possible AI use cases.

Common AI opportunity categories:

#### Knowledge & Search

- Internal knowledge assistant
- Policy Q&A
- Document search
- Technical support assistant
- Contract or compliance lookup

#### Content & Communication

- Drafting emails
- Generating reports
- Summarizing meetings
- Creating marketing content
- Translating or rewriting content

#### Classification & Routing

- Ticket classification
- Email triage
- Lead scoring
- Document categorization
- Risk flagging

#### Extraction & Structuring

- Extracting data from PDFs
- Invoice processing
- Contract metadata extraction
- Resume parsing
- Medical, legal, or financial document review

#### Decision Support

- Forecasting
- Recommendations
- Anomaly detection
- Risk scoring
- Next-best-action suggestions

#### Automation

- AI workflow agents
- Form completion
- CRM updates
- Back-office task automation
- Multi-step process orchestration

#### Customer Experience

- Chatbots
- Virtual agents
- Personalized recommendations
- Customer sentiment analysis
- Voice-of-customer analytics

---

### 5. Data & Systems Assessment

AI depends heavily on data quality, accessibility, and governance.

Assess:

- What data exists?
- Where does it live?
- Is it structured or unstructured?
- Is it clean, labeled, current, and complete?
- Who owns it?
- Can it be accessed securely?
- Are there privacy or regulatory constraints?
- Are APIs available?
- Are documents machine-readable?
- Is there enough historical data?
- Is human feedback available?

Typical data sources:

- CRM
- ERP
- Helpdesk tickets
- Call transcripts
- Emails
- Knowledge bases
- Documents
- Spreadsheets
- Databases
- Product usage logs
- Data warehouses
- Collaboration tools

Important point:

> Some AI use cases can start with limited data using large language models, but production-grade AI still requires strong data governance and integration planning.

---

### 6. Feasibility & Risk Review

Evaluate each use case across technical, organizational, and compliance dimensions.

Questions:

- Can AI solve this well enough?
- Is the task deterministic, judgment-based, or creative?
- What happens if the AI is wrong?
- Is human review required?
- Are there legal, ethical, or safety risks?
- Does the use case involve sensitive data?
- Is explainability required?
- Can the workflow tolerate uncertainty?
- How hard is integration?
- Will users trust the system?

Risk categories:

- Hallucination risk
- Data privacy risk
- Security risk
- Bias/fairness risk
- Compliance risk
- Brand/reputation risk
- Operational dependency risk
- Poor adoption risk
- Integration complexity

A useful rule:

> Favor low-risk, high-value workflows where AI assists humans before fully automating decisions.

---

### 7. Use Case Prioritization

Prioritize opportunities using a scoring model.

Suggested dimensions:

- Business value
- User pain intensity
- Frequency/volume
- Data readiness
- Technical feasibility
- Time to pilot
- Risk level
- Executive sponsorship
- Adoption likelihood
- Measurability

Example scoring matrix:

| Use Case | Value | Feasibility | Risk | Data Readiness | Priority |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| Support ticket summarization | High | High | Low | High | 1 |
| Contract risk review | High | Medium | Medium | Medium | 2 |
| Sales forecasting | Medium | Medium | Low | Medium | 3 |
| Fully autonomous customer service | High | Low | High | Medium | Later |

Good first AI projects usually have:

- Clear ROI
- Manageable scope
- Available data
- Internal users
- Human-in-the-loop review
- Low compliance exposure
- Fast pilot timeline

---

### 8. Prototype / Pilot Definition

For top use cases, define a focused pilot.

A pilot should answer:

- What will be built?
- Who will use it?
- What data will it use?
- What workflow will it fit into?
- What decisions will AI make or assist with?
- What will humans review?
- What does success look like?
- How long will the pilot run?
- What are the go/no-go criteria?

Pilot success metrics:

- Time saved
- Cost reduced
- Accuracy improved
- Error rate reduced
- Customer satisfaction improved
- Employee satisfaction improved
- Throughput increased
- Revenue influenced
- Quality consistency improved

Example pilot definition:

> Build an internal AI assistant for customer support agents that summarizes tickets, retrieves relevant knowledge base articles, and drafts suggested responses. Humans approve all responses before sending. Pilot with 10 agents for 6 weeks. Success = 25% reduction in average handle time and 80% agent satisfaction.

---

### 9. Roadmap & Business Case

After prioritization and pilot scoping, create an AI roadmap.

Roadmap components:

- Ranked use cases
- Pilot plan
- Required data work
- Integration needs
- Technology options
- Governance model
- Security review
- Change management plan
- Budget estimate
- Timeline
- Success metrics
- Scaling approach

The roadmap should distinguish between:

- **Quick wins**
- **Strategic bets**
- **Data foundation work**
- **Governance requirements**
- **Long-term transformation opportunities**

---

### Recommended AI Discovery Workshop Agenda

A useful workshop could run 2–4 hours.

#### Part 1: Business Context

- What are the organization’s top priorities?
- Where are costs, delays, or quality problems?
- What would make the biggest difference this year?

#### Part 2: Workflow Mapping

- Which workflows are manual or repetitive?
- Where do people spend the most time?
- Where does work get stuck?

#### Part 3: Opportunity Brainstorm

- Where could AI assist, augment, or automate?
- What knowledge or data is underused?
- What tasks require reading, writing, classifying, summarizing, or predicting?

#### Part 4: Prioritization

- Which ideas are high-value?
- Which are feasible now?
- Which are too risky or premature?
- Which could become pilots?

#### Part 5: Next Steps

- Select 2–3 top candidates
- Assign owners
- Request data/system access review
- Define pilot scope
- Create business case

---

### Example AI Discovery Questions

#### Business Questions

- What are your biggest operational bottlenecks?
- Which teams are overloaded?
- Where do delays hurt customers or revenue?
- What decisions are slow or inconsistent?
- What would you automate if you could?

#### User Questions

- What tasks do you repeat every day?
- What information do you constantly search for?
- What work feels low-value but necessary?
- Where do mistakes commonly happen?
- What takes longer than it should?

#### Data Questions

- What documents, tickets, calls, or records are generated?
- Is the data digital and accessible?
- Who owns the data?
- Are there sensitive fields?
- Is data quality trusted?
- Are outcomes tracked?

#### Technical Questions

- What systems are involved?
- Are APIs available?
- Is there a data warehouse?
- What identity/security controls exist?
- Can tools be embedded into current workflows?

#### Risk Questions

- What happens if the AI gives a wrong answer?
- Does the use case require explainability?
- Is human approval needed?
- Are there legal or compliance concerns?
- Could the AI expose sensitive information?

---
